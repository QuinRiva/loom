# Shape D in the skills, a density check in the lint, a margin note in the renderer

Three changes, one PR. The documents agents write you take shape D: a verdict line, a table of what each party says with a source column, then short labelled judgements. The source of a claim sits beside it, never inside the sentence. The document check (the render-health lint every agent runs before handing a document back) warns when prose gets dense again.

**The skills.** The files agents read before writing to you gain a worked example in shape D, one rule naming that shape with its reason, and one rule putting the source beside the claim. Their own sentences get shorter.

**The lint.** The check every document passes before you see it gains three warnings: a paragraph over 100 words, a sentence over 30 words, a bracketed citation inside a sentence. Warnings only; nothing fails.

**The renderer.** A new inline tag, `<Sidenote>`, written straight after a sentence. When the panel is wide the note hangs in the right margin beside that line. When the panel is narrow it drops to a small line under the claim. Both states are below.

**Two bets.** An example steers the model more than a rule does, so the example is the load-bearing change. A margin per paragraph beats a page-wide margin, so only a paragraph with a note gives up width.

**Cost.** Three coder threads in parallel and two review gates, about two days. A fourth thread builds a small eval: half a day, about 300k tokens of proxy quota a run.

## 1. The margin note: `<Sidenote>`

<Callout tone="decision" title="An inline tag laid out by CSS, not a wrapper block">
`<Sidenote>` is written inside the paragraph, straight after the sentence it supports, like `<Badge>` is written inside a sentence today. The renderer needs no new layout component: the paragraph that contains a note reserves a right margin for itself, the note floats into it beside the line it follows, and a container query drops the margin when the paragraph's container is narrower than about 42rem. A paragraph without a note is untouched, so no existing document changes.
</Callout>

<Columns>
  <Card eyebrow="Chosen" heading="Inline tag, per-paragraph margin" tone="success" badge="recommended">
    The author writes the note where the claim is, which is the rule the skills teach. The note aligns with the line it follows, Tufte's placement. Only a noted paragraph narrows, so a document with one note keeps its measure everywhere else, and the same CSS works inside a `<Card>` or `<Callout>`.

    Cost: a noted paragraph is narrower than its neighbours, about 40rem of prose instead of 53rem. In a shape-D unit most judgement paragraphs carry a source, so the ragged edge is confined to the unit.
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="Rejected" heading="A wrapper block that holds the paragraph and its notes" tone="neutral" badge="rejected">
    `<Noted>…<Note>…</Note></Noted>` around each paragraph, laid out as a two-column grid like the `<Steps>` aside track. Robust, but the author writes a container per paragraph, the notes stack at the top of the paragraph rather than beside their lines, and it is a second way to say the same thing as the inline tag.
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="Rejected" heading="A page-wide margin" tone="neutral" badge="rejected">
    Narrow every paragraph whenever the document has a note and line the notes up in one column. Consistent, but one note reshapes the whole document, and the panel beside chat is often narrower than the prose measure, so there is no margin to reserve. The brief dropped it and you chose the per-paragraph note.
  </Card>
</Columns>

### 1.1 The contract

- **Tag.** `Sidenote`, no attributes. The text between the tags is the note: inline markdown, so a code span like `F07:…`, a link or _emphasis_ render. Registered as `inline: true`, like `<Badge>`, so the generated schema reference describes it as text inside a sentence, not a block.
- **Where it may sit.** Inside a paragraph, after the full stop of the sentence it supports. One sentence may carry one note; a paragraph may carry several, which stack down the margin in order. Inside a `<Card>`, `<Callout>` or `<Column>` body it works the same, against that body's width. In a table cell it does not parse (cells are string attributes, as for `<Badge>`); the table's source column is the place there. In a tight list item there is no paragraph to reserve a margin, so the note always renders as a line under the item; the vocabulary entry says so. On its own line between paragraphs it renders as a small line, and the lint warns.
- **Wide rendering.** The paragraph reserves 13rem on its right; the note is 12rem wide, small muted text at the `######` line's size, floated right and cleared so a second note sits under the first. It aligns with the top of the line box its tag sits in.
- **Narrow rendering.** When the nearest container (the document root, or a card or callout body) is narrower than the breakpoint, the paragraph gives up the margin and the note becomes a block line in its place in the text: directly under the sentence it follows. That is the brief's "small source line directly under the paragraph", placed at the claim rather than at the paragraph's end, as your answer to the brief says ("a small line under the claim").
- **Annotation.** A note is prose, not a block: selecting its text leaves a text-quote comment on the note itself. The flattened text the anchoring code searches gets a line break before a note, so a quote of the sentence before the note never runs into the note's words. Comment badges keep parking outside the prose column, because the margin is inside the paragraph's own box.
- **Not in the first cut.** A marker numeral in the sentence linking it to its note. The note sits beside its line when wide and under it when narrow, so position carries the link; a numeral is a second cut if a paragraph with three notes proves hard to read.

### 1.3 Risks

- `container-type: inline-size` applies layout containment, which makes the root a stacking context and a containing block for positioned descendants. It is declared only when a note is present and `<Steps>` already uses it on itself, but two fixtures are re-run with a note added: `mdx-annotation-defects`, to confirm highlights still track on resize, and the "Plan document" bleed fixture, because layout containment turns the root's visible overflow into ink overflow and a wide block that spills past the root is where that would show.
- `:has()` is already relied on by the steps rail, so no new browser floor.
- Three or more notes on one short paragraph overflow the paragraph's height; `display: flow-root` makes the paragraph grow to hold them, which is the right failure.

## 2. The density check

Three warnings, never errors, computed on the parsed tree the lint already walks. They go in a new pure module, `apps/web/src/components/files/mdx-plan/proseDensity.ts`, so the eval in §4 can score model output with the same code. Its interface, fixed here because the lint and eval coders work in parallel:

- **Input.** An MDX source string. The module parses it itself with `unified().use(remarkParse).use(remarkFrontmatter).use(remarkGfm).use(remarkMdx)`, the same chain `planLint.ts` uses, and also exports that parse as `parseProseTree(source)` so `planLint.ts` can hand over the tree it already has instead of parsing twice (`proseDensity(treeOrSource)` accepts either).
- **Output.** One record, not messages: `{ longParagraphs: { line, words }[], longSentences: { line, words }[], bracketedCitations: { line }[], ownLineSidenotes: { line }[] }`, with the thresholds exported as `PARAGRAPH_WORDS = 100` and `SENTENCE_WORDS = 30`. `planLint.ts` turns the record into the grouped warning messages below; the eval counts the arrays.
- **Imports.** Only `unified`, `remark-parse`, `remark-frontmatter`, `remark-gfm` and `remark-mdx`. No import from `planLint.ts` or `registry.tsx` (they pull in React), and no relative import at all, so the eval, which runs as `node run.ts` with type stripping and needs explicit `.ts` extensions, can import the file by path (`../../apps/web/src/components/files/mdx-plan/proseDensity.ts`) and typecheck it under `evals/tsconfig.json`. Those five packages are added to `evals/package.json` so the import resolves there.

**What is not detectable, and is not pretended.** Facts per paragraph (the specimen's nine), terms used before they are explained (its seven), and a citation sitting between a subject and its verb all need a reader. The checks above catch the mechanical signatures that travelled with those; the exemplar and the rule carry the rest. Models follow numeric limits poorly (the research's LIFEBench finding), so the thresholds are a backstop for the reviewer and the author's own pass, not the lever.

**Sentence splitting.** Text of a paragraph is built from its `text` nodes, with each inline code span replaced by one token so a path's dots are not sentence ends. Sentences end at `.`, `!` or `?` followed by whitespace and a capital, opening quote or bracket; `e.g.`, `i.e.`, `vs.`, `etc.` and `§4.1` are not ends. Words are whitespace-separated tokens. Skipped entirely: headings (so `######` source lines never fire), blockquotes, table cells, code, and the children of a `<Sidenote>`.

**Output shape.** One finding per check per document, listing the lines, so a long plan gets at most four density lines rather than a flood that buries the structural warnings:

### 3.2 The register pass

Measured with the script below (which splits bullet lists into their items; the research's "median 28, a tenth past 64" figure read a list as one sentence), the five files run at a median of 22 to 27 words a sentence, a tenth past 43 to 51, and longest sentences of 62 to 121. The vendor's guidance is that prompt style may carry into output. The pass applies to the five prose files in the table and nothing else (`block-vocabulary.md`, `wireframe.md`, `canvas.md` and the generated `block-schema.md` stay). Its rules for the coder:

- Split a sentence over 30 words at its clause joint, and keep the "because", "so" or "unless" that joined the halves. A newcomer to a term needs the link stated (the research's McNamara finding); bare fragments are the other failure.
- A rule and its reason are two sentences, rule first.
- An em-dash aside becomes its own sentence or is cut.
- No new rule enters. A sentence that only restates a rule the same file already holds, or that `block-schema.md` makes derivable, is cut; that is where the net reduction comes from.
- A rule stated in both a genre `SKILL.md` and `document-quality.md` keeps the one in `document-quality.md` and a pointer in the skill.

Target, measured by the coder and re-measured by the reviewer with the same script: in each of the five files, median sentence at most 20 words and no sentence over 40; the five files' total word count down, and each file's own count down except `exemplar.md`, which gains the fact-dense card and may end net up by at most the card's size. `lint-doc.sh` on the card pasted into a scratch `.mdx` passes only once the renderer thread has registered `Sidenote`, so that check runs at gate 2, not in the skills thread.

### 3.3 The reviewer's brief for the skills gate

The gate reviewer receives: the purpose sentence per file (`document-quality.md`: what does not vary across genres, who the document is for, how it is ordered, how judgement and evidence are shown, and the check before handoff; `exemplar.md`: the order a good document of each genre reads in, worked, and the anti-patterns with their cost; each genre `SKILL.md`: when that genre is the document, where it goes, its skeleton and its discipline), the per-file counts, the measurement output before and after, the four rejection reasons as a veto, and the eval's run (§4). The reviewer also reads the exemplar card as its reader would and says whether it can be repeated after one pass; that is the test the research set.

## 4. The eval

A density eval answers the brief's open question, whether register transfers, with data instead of a reading. You asked for it to run before the skills reviewer signs off. It follows `evals/README.md`'s shape.

- `evals/explainer-density/`: `run.ts`, `README.md`, `cases.ts`, a row in the evals table.
- **One case.** An invented fact pack in the specimen's shape (a clause, a field definition, a contract, a gold answer, a model answer, a scoring rule, a counterfactual, a neighbouring row: nine facts, three parties) and the instruction to write that section of an explainer for the maintainer. No real data, per the evals rules.
- **The prompt.** System: the explainer skill, `document-quality.md` and `exemplar.md`, read from a git ref given as `--skills-ref` (`git show <ref>:<path>`). User: the fact pack. Model: `claude-fable-5-1`, the default in Carl's pi settings and the model whose density is the thing measured, through the CLIProxyAPI route `evals/shared/proxy.ts` already provides, so a run costs proxy quota rather than a key; `--model` overrides.
- **Arms, nested so each delta is attributable.** The skills coder lands two commits, in this order: first the rule, the exemplar card and the §4 row; then the register pass. The arms are then three refs: the branch's merge-base with `main` (today's skills), the first commit, and the second. Five samples per arm, cache-busted, plus a second five-sample run of the first arm as the A/A noise floor.
- **Grader.** `proseDensity.ts` from §2 (paragraphs over 100, sentences over 30, bracketed citations), plus mean sentence length and whether the output has a verdict line, a `<Table>` and bold-labelled paragraphs, as plain regexes. `run.test.ts` tests the grader. A person reads the samples that moved.
- **Cost.** About 20 calls (four five-sample runs) of 15k input tokens, so about 300k tokens of proxy quota a run; recorded in the summary.

The eval thread runs it once the skills thread's two commits and the lint module are on the branch, before gate 1, and the gate reads its summary. The drop rule, scored: the primary metric is mean sentence length per output, the secondary the count of sentences over 30 words. The register pass stays if the third arm lowers the primary metric against the second by more than the A/A spread (the difference between the two first-arm runs) and is not worse on the secondary; otherwise it is dropped by reverting its commit, and the brief's second answer is reported back as "measured, no effect beyond noise". If the two arms sit inside the spread but the direction is right, the eval is rerun at ten samples before the rule is applied.
