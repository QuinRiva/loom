# Fact-dense explanations: lead with the verdict, put the parties in a table, then label each judgement

**Recommendation: shape D.** A verdict line comes first. A small table then shows what each party says, with its source in its own column. Each judgement follows as a short labelled paragraph: why it failed, what would have happened otherwise, whose miss it is, and what happened next door.

**Two reasons.** First, a reader holds about four things at once, and the original paragraph asks for nine. The table holds the facts so the reader does not have to. Second, the fields that live on dense, cited argument all do the same two things. They keep the source out of the sentence, and they separate facts from judgements, each under its own label.

Below is the real paragraph you were reading, rewritten in shape D. Every fact in the original is still there. Judge it by reading it.

### 1.1 The limit is items held, not words read

Cowan's review puts the central limit at three to five meaningful items. Miller's famous "seven" was, in Cowan's words, "a rough estimate and a rhetorical device".

###### [Cowan 2001](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/magical-number-4-in-shortterm-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496)

The specimen's second sentence asks you to hold three of them at once. Gold's reading, the definition's wording and the contract's keys arrive in one sentence, joined by a semicolon and a comma.

Gibson's work explains why distance hurts. Each word that must be joined to an earlier one costs more the further back that word is. A sentence that opens "Had it written the value" leaves a thread open until its last clause.

###### [Gibson 2000, the dependency locality theory](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_2000_DLT.pdf)

### 1.2 Splitting has a cost too

Short sentences only help if the links between them survive. McNamara and colleagues found that readers new to a subject learned more from a text that stated its connections. Readers who already knew the subject did better with a sparser one.

###### [McNamara, Kintsch, Songer and Kintsch 1996](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1)

You are the first kind of reader for most of what agents explain to you. The jargon is new even when the project is not. So the rewrite keeps "because", "so" and "even then", and glosses each term the first time it appears.

Tufte makes the same point from the other side. His case against slide bullets is that they break reasoning into pieces and lose the links between them. A table of facts with no sentence explaining it repeats that mistake.

###### [Tufte, *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint*](https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/the-cognitive-style-of-powerpoint-pitching-out-corrupts-within-ebook/)

### 1.3 How fields that live on cited argument do it

<Columns columns={2}>
  <Card eyebrow="Law" heading="Citations out of the sentence; facts, issue and ruling under separate heads" tone="info">
    Bryan Garner noticed two appeal judges who moved every citation out of the text. Their opinions, he wrote, "actually had a discernible train of thought". His fallback for in-text citing is a separate citation sentence. The US Federal Judicial Center tells judges to lay out issues, facts, discussion and disposition as separate parts.

    ###### [Garner, "The Citational Footnote"](https://scribes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Scribes_vol7_14_The_Citational_Footnote.pdf); [Garner, ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/magazine/article/end_weight_sentence_emphasis_garner); [FJC, *Judicial Writing Manual*](https://www.fjc.gov/content/judicial-writing-manual-pocket-guide-judges-second-edition)
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="Intelligence analysis" heading="Facts kept apart from judgements; the bottom line first" tone="info">
    The US analytic standards require a product to "clearly distinguish statements that convey underlying intelligence information" from "assumptions or judgments". The US Army's writing standard asks for the main point first, so the text is understood "in a single rapid reading".

    ###### [ICD 203, standard 3](https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf); [AR 25-50, para 1-38](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN42124-AR_25-50-007-WEB-13.pdf)
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="Structured writing" heading="One point per chunk, and every chunk labelled" tone="info">
    Information Mapping's rules are to chunk, keep one main point per chunk, and label every chunk. Its inventor, Robert Horn, called labelling "the single change that would improve written communication to the greatest extent". Minto's pyramid adds the order: the answer first, then the grouped support.

    ###### [Horn, "What Kinds of Writing Have a Future?"](https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/TC510-Fall2011/Horn-DocsWithFuture.pdf); [Minto, via Toolshero](https://www.toolshero.com/communication-methods/minto-pyramid-principle/)
  </Card>
  <Card eyebrow="Scientific writing" heading="Subject next to its verb; the new fact at the end of the sentence" tone="info">
    Gopen and Swan show readers stumble when many words separate a subject from its verb. Readers also expect the new information at the end of a sentence, its "stress position". One new fact per sentence gives each fact that position.

    ###### [Gopen and Swan 1990, *American Scientist*](https://www.americanscientist.org/blog/the-long-view/the-science-of-scientific-writing)
  </Card>
</Columns>

The four fields agree on two moves. The source leaves the sentence. Facts and judgements sit apart, each under a label.

### 1.4 Where citations and examples go

Garner's footnotes and the split-attention research pull in opposite directions. Footnotes clear the sentence but send the eye to the bottom of the page. Chandler and Sweller found that separated sources cost the reader effort to join.

Tufte's sidenotes resolve this: the note sits in the margin beside its line. The panel has no sidenote block. Its footnotes work, but they render at the very end of the document.

###### [Tufte CSS on sidenotes](https://edwardtufte.github.io/tufte-css/); [Chandler and Sweller 1991](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0804_2)

*Vocabulary gap: a margin note beside a line was wanted. A source column, or a small source line directly under the paragraph (this document's `######` lines), stands in for it.*

Examples follow the same rule. The quote goes on its own line, its source on the line under it, and the claim it supports in the sentence just before. In shape D the quote is the licence row of the table.

Shape C needs no blocks beyond bold labels, so it works in a chat reply too. Each judgement is findable by its label. Its cost is the four "what X says" units read in sequence where a table would set them side by side.

### 2.4 Why D over C

D and C share the verdict line and the labelled judgements. D adds one thing: the parties' readings sit in one table, each with its source in a column.

That table is where the specimen's difficulty lived. Your words were "relationships between those facts". A table shows a relationship between rows without a sentence having to state it, and the source column keeps the citation beside the claim.

D costs the most words. Most of the extra is labels, the gloss line and the table's headers. A scanning reader reads those first and dips into the text under them: NN/g's eyetracking calls this the layer-cake pattern, and it is the pattern labels exist to serve.

###### [NN/g, The layer-cake pattern of scanning](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)

When a unit has only one party, the table adds nothing. Use C there. A rule of thumb: two or more parties saying something about the same fact earn a table.

Read together, these point at three levers rather than one. An exemplar written in the target shape shows the model what to produce. A short rule, phrased as what to do and with its reason, names the shape. A mechanical check catches what neither guarantees, since a "25 words" rule is the kind of number models miss.

The register point matters most for you. The skills' own prose runs at a median of 31 to 33 words a sentence. If prompt style carries into output, a rule about short sentences written in long sentences works against itself.

## 4. Where the evidence is thin

The most quoted number on this topic is the American Press Institute finding: 8-word sentences fully understood, 43-word sentences under 10%. It appears on hundreds of style blogs. I could not trace it to a published study, so this document does not rely on it.

###### Example of the claim: [Wylie Communications](https://www.wyliecomm.com/how-long-should-a-sentence-be/)

No study I found tests whether an instruction's sentence length transfers to an LLM's output. The "match your prompt style" advice is Anthropic's, observed on formatting such as markdown, not measured on sentence length.

The comprehension research is mostly on students and general readers, not on experts reading technical reports. McNamara's result suggests experts tolerate density better. You are an expert in the project but a newcomer to each document's coined terms, so the newcomer findings apply to the terms.

The four shapes have not been tested on you. The quickest test is the one this document already makes. Read the four renderings of §3.3. Which one could you explain to someone else after one pass?
