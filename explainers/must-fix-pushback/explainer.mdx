# Coders contest 7% of must-fixes, and most must-fixes are real fixes

Across every review-gate rework round since gates began, coders pushed back on **86 of 1,177 must-fix findings (7%)**, in 72 of 495 rounds. Reviewers accepted the coder's reasons in 55 of those 86 and contested once in 27. Judged against the reviewer role's own severity bar, **12 of a random 192 must-fixes (6%) were clearly not required fixes**, another 13 were borderline, and the rest were real bugs, false claims or unmet brief requirements. Of the 12 that were not required, the coder implemented **7 without pushback**. Nearly all were one-line wording or constant changes that cost almost nothing.

<Callout focus title="What it means now">
The permission to reject a finding is already written in six places and coders visibly use it: 87% of the must-fixes they implemented carry a stated reason, often in its exact vocabulary ("Accepted. I checked the three reasons", "Rejected, with reasons"). The must-fixes they accept are overwhelmingly genuine. **Recommendation: no change to the coder or reviewer role text.** The residual reflex (cheap compliance with roughly 4% of must-fixes) costs little, and adding a sentence would not change the economics behind it. Two things are worth watching instead: pushback fell from 10% to 4% when the coders moved to Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 while the share of bar-failing findings did not move, and reviewers skip the recovery-cost half of the severity bar on 56% of must-fixes.
</Callout>

### What cheap compliance sounds like

When a coder has weighed a finding, judged it not strictly needed and done it anyway, it usually says why, and the reason is nearly always that compliance costs less than another round. A behaviour-parity review: "functionally this was already neutral ... But strict parity honours the brief's behaviour-preservation mandate and removes all doubt, so it was worth doing." <small>(Source: `80da4127-8402-4c26-9107-6e0b44412752.round-1.md` and the coder's `5d13cc24-2cc5-469d-96b2-e93307e740eb.round-1.md`, 8 Jul.)</small> A Slack-bridge review, after the reviewer upheld its finding once: "I still don't think my threat-model reasoning was wrong on its own terms. But a governing brief constraint doesn't need to win that argument to be binding, and the cost of compliance here turned out to be near-zero." <small>(Source: `20d19b49-34d4-4264-bf66-fcf4ef260cfc.round-2.md`, 21 Aug.)</small> In November the cheap cases are mostly prose trims labelled as must-fix in reviews of instruction text, for example "delete '`goal_task_list` reads the tree in full.'", which the coder simply deleted. <small>(Source: Intent brief review gate, 2 Nov, finding 9.)</small>

### The one costly variant: complying against your own evidence

<Card eyebrow="Origin provenance review · 13 Jul · loom" heading="The coder found the hazard in the reviewer's fix, recorded it, and shipped the fix anyway" tone="risk" badge="RARE · 13 OF 1,177 DISAGREED BUT COMPLIED">
  The reviewer: "Migration number `054` already collides with the concurrently developed fork feature ... Renumber message provenance (expected next slot: `055`)."

  The coder renumbered, then wrote: "Coordination caveat I want on record: this repo's migrator applies only migrations with `id > latest-applied`; it does not backfill gaps out of order ... (e.g. a DB that already applied `055` before `054` exists would skip `054`)."

  The reviewer, next round: "Migration `055` is not safe while `054` is absent. Your newly discovered migrator behaviour confirms the problem ... Keep this branch contiguous (use 54 here)."

  The coder's own evidence was a reason to reject; it treated it as a caveat instead. The loop caught it a round later, so nothing shipped broken. <small>(Source: `a36c3a47-2731-4082-8848-854161035b32.round-2.md`; coder `cbec5e33-b03d-466e-b627-6d0fb1e021a8.round-1.md`.)</small>

### What happens when a coder does push back

<Steps>
  <Step at="23 Jul" title="Coder holds the brief's literal wording; reviewer withdraws" tone="success" badge="ACCEPTED">
    Coder: "The brief says something different, verbatim ... I'm holding the brief's literal." Reviewer: "Finding 1 is withdrawn after consulting the orchestrator; the coder's cap formula is correct."
  </Step>
  <Step at="26 Jul" title="Coder rejects twice; parent rules for the coder" tone="success" badge="ESCALATED · CODER RIGHT">
    Coder: "That guard is a workaround for the exact defect this change removes." Reviewer: "I accept the technical substance of your objection ... However, this review cannot waive an explicit acceptance criterion." After the second contest the gate escalated, and the parent "explicitly waived importing `FIELDS_EXCLUDED_FROM_ADJUDICATION`: it is an obsolete sibling-branch workaround."
  </Step>
  <Step at="21 Aug" title="Coder rejects; reviewer cites a constraint only its own brief had" tone="warning" badge="CONTESTED ONCE · CODER COMPLIED">
    Coder: "I can't find the premise ... the brief says nothing about logging, identifiers or redaction." Reviewer: "The review-gate brief explicitly says to check that 'no credential or channel-id values leak into logs' ... That constraint was absent from the coder's original implementation brief."
  </Step>
</Steps>

The third case is a pattern rather than a one-off: 12 of the 27 re-contests cite the reviewer's brief, an acceptance contract or a signed design. The reviewer is enforcing an acceptance criterion the coder may never have been shown. That is an orchestrator briefing habit, not a role-language problem.

    ["Working as intended, must-fixes genuinely required", { "badge": { "label": "mostly", "tone": "success" }, "md": "" }, "87% of sampled must-fixes met the bar on a hand check; outright rejections land on bad findings 13 times in 14; reviewers accept 64% of pushback."]

**The reviewer half of the bar is the weak link, not the coder half.** Reviewers state a concrete consequence for 89% of must-fixes but a recovery cost for only 44%, so the "if you can't, it isn't must-fix" test is mostly not applied. It does filter when applied: findings that state both fail the sceptical judge 10% of the time (9 of 92), findings that do not fail it 20% of the time (20 of 100).

**The contested field does not measure pushback.** Only 2 of 495 coder rework submits used it; coders push back in their round report's prose, as their role tells them to. Most of the 19 submits that carry `contested` are reviewers recording their own re-contests, as the gate skill instructs. A dashboard reading that field would show almost no pushback.

## 5. Recommendation: no change to the role text

<Callout tone="decision" title="Leave the coder and reviewer language as it is">
The sentence you would add already exists and is being read. The cases where coders comply with a not-required must-fix are cheap edits whose cost is a few words; adding another bullet to the coder role would add clutter to fix a few-word problem, and would not change the economics (one more round versus one more edit) that produce it. The one dangerous variant, complying against your own evidence, occurred once with real stakes in 1,177 findings and was caught by the loop.
</Callout>

If you want to act anyway, these are the only levers the evidence supports, in order of value:

- **Re-run this measurement in a month** rather than editing text. The drop from 10% to 4% coincides with the move to Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 coders, under unchanged text, and the not-required share did not fall with it. If newer models are more compliant, a text change would be an experiment against that, and this pipeline (section 6) re-derives the numbers in about twenty minutes.
- **Tell orchestrators to give the coder the gate's acceptance criteria.** Several re-contests are the reviewer enforcing a criterion only its own brief carried. That belongs in the orchestrator's gate-spawn habit, not in the coder role.
- **Leave the reviewer severity bar alone too.** The recovery-cost clause halves the bad-finding rate when reviewers follow it, but it is already explicit; a louder version is the prescriptive clutter you are trying to remove.

## 6. How the numbers were made, and their limits

<Details summary="Method">
**Corpus.** Every `thread.outcome-recorded` event with outcome `needs_rework` in the live ledger, read-only, paired with the `thread.route-taken` hop it caused to find the reopened thread, that thread's next submit (its round report) and the reviewer's next verdict. 571 verdicts, 526 reopened a thread, 522 had a coder report on disk. 27 were a coder submitting the `needs_rework` token back to its reviewer and are excluded, leaving 495 rounds.

**Per-finding classification.** Gemini (`gemini-4-argon`) read each round's three reports and returned one row per must-fix: a literal quote, the kind, whether the reviewer stated a consequence and a recovery cost, a meets-the-bar judgement, the coder's action, whether it showed evaluation, and the reviewer's adjudication. It found 1,177 must-fixes where reviewers' `counts.mustFix` totals 1,166.

**Bar judgement, three passes.** That first judge rated 95% as meeting the bar, which is lenient because it reads the reviewer's framing. A second, sceptical judge (`gemini-3.1-pro-preview`, counterfactual question, reviewer's label hidden in the wording) re-judged a seeded random 192, and rated 29 not required. I then read those 29 myself: 12 clearly not required, 13 borderline, 4 actually required (an unformatted file failing `vp check`, unreadable 8px diagrams in a talk, a forced branch delete that could lose a grandchild's commits, a garbled legal sentence in a client document). The headline 12 of 192 is my count; the 95% confidence range on 6% is about 4% to 11%.

**Known gaps.** Model is the one recorded at thread creation. 28 coder reports sit in files a later submit could have overwritten. The July 3 rounds include early gate bugs (one reviewer resubmitted its round-1 report verbatim in round 2). Must-fixes raised outside gates, in plain review reports to an orchestrator, are not counted.
</Details>
