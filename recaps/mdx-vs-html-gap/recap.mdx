# Why the HTML explainers read better than our MDX plans and recaps

Most of the gap is **who wrote the document, for whom, and how it was told to open** — not what the MDX renderer can draw. Every genuine HTML page was written by a thread that had been talking to its reader for hours and was asked, in the reader's own words, to "help me understand"; five of the six MDX documents were written by a child thread from a brief, for a reader it never met, whose job was to approve or decide. The MDX skill then tells that child to open with the review protocol and the work objective, in the codebase's vocabulary, with prose by default and emphasis banned. The renderer adds a real but bounded second layer: no inline pill, no number strip, no tone in a table cell, a 20px title over 16px body text. Roughly: **the writing ~45%, the writer's situation ~25%, the medium ~30%**. The two best MDX recaps in the sample already read nearly as well as the HTML, using today's vocabulary.

## 1. The causes, ranked

| # | Cause | Kind | Share | Where the fix lives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Writer's situation and task genre.** HTML: a root thread in conversation with the reader, asked to explain. MDX: a child from a brief, never met the reader, asked to produce a decision gate. | Confound — strong | ~25% | The orchestrator's brief; an explainer genre |
| 2 | **The first screen carries the work objective or the review protocol, not the answer.** | Authoring, caused by instruction | ~18% | Skill skeletons |
| 3 | **Devices the vocabulary lacks:** inline pill, number strip, toned table cells, proportion bar, stepper. | Medium — vocabulary | ~18% | New blocks |
| 4 | **Insider register and code narration:** thread ids, "M4", "D2 item 2 of the July 6 plan", no worked example. | Authoring, caused by instruction | ~15% | A reader step; gloss rule for body prose |
| 5 | **Judgement written as sentences where a badge or tone already exists.** | Authoring | ~12% | Doctrine and exemplars |
| 6 | **Flat rendering:** 20px title over 16px body, no section rule or nav, grids squeezed to one column width, 10px grey chrome. | Medium — renderer CSS | ~12% | One stylesheet |

The shares are a judgement from reading all ten artefacts, not a measurement. The model is **not** a cause: the same Fable and Opus generations wrote both sides. How strong is cause 1? Role and briefing split the sample almost perfectly, so this corpus cannot separate "MDX is worse" from "children writing cold for a decision write worse". Two counterpoints keep it honest: the one HTML page a root synthesised from child reports with no human request is the one Carl rejected; and the MDX plans did their decision job — the forms were answered and the work shipped.

Each intervention in section 3 has an Accept / Reject / Discuss control; one left undecided takes its stated recommendation.

### 2.3 Devices the vocabulary lacks

Of the twelve devices that carry most of the meaning in the four HTML pages, **three are native** in MDX (toned callout, collapsible drill-down, before/after columns), **five are degraded** (card grids, stepper, pipeline, tables with meaning in colour, title and section hierarchy) and **four are impossible** with the documented vocabulary: inline status pills, the number strip, the state-coloured matrix and timeline, and proportion bars. Pills are the one device all four pages lean on hardest — in sentences, table cells, headings and legends. The strip at the top of this document is what reaching for the escape hatch costs.

### 2.4 Insider register

  <Column label="MDX recap, the answer paragraph">

  </Column>
  <Column label="HTML explainer, the opening">
    > Your v1 system answers *"what type is this file?"* — flat per-document labels, per company, via a LangGraph of prompt/invoke/parse nodes. That leaves downstream consumers (extraction, audit) to *guess* at everything relational…
  </Column>

The instruction behind the left column is the recap skeleton: *"A 1–3 paragraph outcome narrative — what changed and why, in the codebase's vocabulary."* Nothing in the MDX pipeline asks who the reader is or what they already know; the only gloss rule ("never leave a label the document coined … unglossed") applies to the question form, not the body. Carl has asked for the missing thing directly in past sessions: *"walk me through with examples… these abstract explanations are meaningless to me."*

### 2.5 Judgement as sentences

One row of a six-option weighing table in an MDX plan, as authored:

> "weak: colour-only, and the title already encodes unread / receded / active by colour and weight", "collides with the title's existing state styling", "no (tooltip could add it)", "trivial, web only"

The same job in the HTML orientation page is a column of `OURS` / `THEIRS` / `BOTH` pills and an evidence footer per card. Half of this is authoring: `Card` already has a `badge` and `meta` chips, and the two best MDX recaps use them exactly this way (`badge="RECOMMENDED · fix now"`, `meta` of `"client only", "no new RPC", "removes ~91% of false chips"`). The other half is medium: a table cell cannot hold a pill. Here is the nearest this vocabulary gets —

### 2.6 Flat rendering

This section's heading is the evidence: in the stylesheet, a document title is 1.25rem, a section heading 1.125rem and a sub-heading 1rem, all over 16px body text, with no rule between sections and no generated outline. The HTML pages run 30–34px titles and 21–24px section heads over 15px body, with numbered `01…06` heads and a sticky section nav. Card badges and meta chips are 10px grey, so status reads as metadata rather than signal. Prose and every container block sit in a ~848px column — four cards side by side are impossible, while the HTML pages use 1060–1180px.

Grouped by cost. Each card says what changes for you as a reader, the rough effort, and a recommendation.

## 4. The cheapest test that would prove this

One child thread, one brief, two documents. Reuse the brief that produced the fan-in deadlock recap (the most insider-heavy MDX in the sample) with three added lines — who reads it, what they know, what they must be able to say after the first screen. The child writes the HTML page with the visual-explainer skill and the MDX recap with today's vocabulary under a one-page draft of the reader-brief and answer-first rules. You then have three documents on one subject.

Existing MDX against new MDX isolates the instruction effect (same role, same medium). New MDX against new HTML isolates the medium (same writer context). What the cold-written HTML loses against the four root-written pages is the writer's-situation effect. Cost: about an hour of child time and a short reading pass. If the new MDX closes most of the distance, the cheap tier suffices and renderer work can follow at leisure; if not, the residual names which blocks matter. Zero-cost fallback: next time you ask a root for a "help me understand" page, have it produce the MDX too.

This document is a smaller version of the same test — written under the proposed rules with today's vocabulary and one escape-hatch figure. Where it could not do what the HTML pages do, the text says so in place.
