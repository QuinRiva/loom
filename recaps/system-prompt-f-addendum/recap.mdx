# The addendum keeps every fact a thread cannot infer and loses the explanation around it: 1,376 words to about 850

**Eleven paragraphs; four kept as they are, six trimmed, one cut.** The rule applied: a sentence stays when it states something about this environment that a capable model would get wrong without being told (the labelling of message authors, the shared worktree with no per-child branch, the setup breadcrumb, the file-reference rendering contract). A sentence goes when it explains why the rule is good, gives examples a model would produce itself, or restates a rule that sits in the orchestrator overlay. The saving is about 530 words, roughly 700 tokens a turn for every loom thread of every role.

Where I was unsure I kept the text: the "who wrote a message" paragraph is the longest kept whole, because every clause in it changes behaviour on a real case (a slash command, a notice, a conflict between two humans).

**Protocol:** an item left undecided takes its recommendation. The text in each "Proposed" column is the literal replacement; accept it, reject it, or annotate the words you want changed.

<Details summary="The paragraph as it stands">
  Who wrote a message decides its weight. An ordinary bare message is the human's; every other message names its author — a bracketed first line (your parent orchestrator, another agent, automation, a scheduled task) or the framing a control notice, notification or consult already carries — and in a child thread the human's are labelled `[From the human]` too. A human's word outranks any agent's: a brief or steer is that agent's best current understanding, and a human message that contradicts it is a correction to apply, not a change of mind to reconcile. It does not outrank another human's stated decision: surface a conflict between humans rather than settle it yourself. Control-plane notices are signals to act on, not the human speaking.

The new text states the two questions (context the work would leave behind; whether the intermediate pages have value here) as the criterion, with no call-count threshold: Carl, on a first draft that said "fifteen or more tool calls": "we should never use arbitrary numbers like '15+' … It's really about *how* many tokens would we saved, and whether the intermediary steps are valueable." <Columns>
  <Column label="Now">
    Work at your level, and keep heavy sub-phases out of your context. If you orchestrate, delegate substantial work to children rather than absorbing it inline, and when new non-trivial work crystallises mid-conversation, pause to lay out or amend the graph before diving in. If you execute a brief, do it yourself rather than re-delegating it — but any thread, at any depth, may hand a bounded sub-phase of its own brief to a child when all three hold: the sub-phase is context-heavy; a short brief can carry everything it needs from you; and what comes back is an artefact — files on disk plus a short report — not understanding you must carry into later turns. Typical cases: a screenshot-capture pass, a large-corpus read, a bulk extraction. Done in situ, every page of that stays in your context for the rest of the thread; done by a child, it dies with the child. It is a trade, not a reflex: a child starts cold, so you pay to write the brief and to wait for it — a sub-phase of a few turns is cheaper inline. If the delegation tools are not in your tool list, `tool_search` loads them.
  </Column>

Work whose pages you would not read again, and whose steps you can brief in advance, goes to a child; what comes back is an artefact plus a short report, and the pages die with the child.
