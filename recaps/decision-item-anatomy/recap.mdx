# A decision card answers the reader's questions in the order they ask them

**Question, answer, facts, rule, readings, change, cost of inaction — then the evidence folds away.** Each part is there because you asked for it, in that order, while reading Decision B: *what is this about → what is the lapsed-cover rule → what do these dates mean → is this stale → where does this come from → what did each side conclude and why → where do the three lines go.* The card you read held every one of those answers in nine sentences and a diff whose panels were two model names; it made you reconstruct the case. The rewrite in §2 puts the facts in fields with their source, labels the two readings as readings, names the file the three lines go into, and says plainly what nobody recorded (whether the documents were refreshed).

## 1. The anatomy, and why this order

The order is your trace, read as a list of questions. You read the title and got "a vague idea" — so the heading is the question itself. You tried the paragraph and gave up — so no paragraph: the facts are rows with a source column, because six of your fourteen questions were *where does this come from* or *is this stale*. You re-read the dates three times — so every date carries its gloss ("ended 2 days before the audit date"). You asked "what is the lapsed-cover rule" in the first sentence — so the rule is quoted, after the facts and before the readings, where you need it to follow them. You read the `FieldDiff` as before/after — so two parties' conclusions are a table whose column labels name the party, its model, its date and what it was looking at; `FieldDiff` stays for a genuine before/after (as keyed → recommended). You asked "where are they being added, what is a row" — so the change names the file, and a word that means two things in one card (the key's *row*, the rule's *row*) is glossed or replaced.

<Details summary="The reasoning in full: budgets, what the outside formats agree on, and where the hypothesis was tested and changed">
  **Question before answer, both above the fold.** The brief's hypothesis put the question first and left open whether the recommendation precedes it. Both fit in the card header: the heading is the question, the badge is the recommended answer, and the body's first line is the answer with its reason. A reader who trusts the writer accepts from the header; a reader who does not has the question framed before the facts arrive. Putting the recommendation in the heading instead (as the original did — "check the cover class before the period") reads as a title for a topic, which is what produced "a vague idea".

  **Facts as fields, with a source column, and unknowns stated.** The original's second sentence onward is a fact table told as prose; every reader re-derives it. A table is read once. The source column is the direct answer to "is it from the PDF or from the extraction?", "did we sync the documents?", "is this a known exception?" — and where the pack does not say, the cell says *not recorded*, because an honest unknown is what you wanted and it stops the reader hunting.

  **Rule after facts, before readings.** IRAC — the case-brief form (issue, rule, application, conclusion), with the material facts stated before the issue in its FIRAC variant — and the one-page decision memo (decision requested → recommendation → facts → options → risks) agree on the one thing that matters here: the facts are separated from anyone's analysis of them, the question is stated as a question, and the action asked for is literal. The readings are applications of the rule to the facts, so the rule precedes them. Quoting the operative line costs two lines and removes the reader's first unknown.

  **Readings are not a diff.** `FieldDiff` renders two panels; a reader meets them as before/after whatever the labels say, and the original's panels were "3.1 Pro history" and "Key (as keyed)" — two readings. A `<Table>` with the parties as columns carries the same cells with no implied direction, and the column label has room for the party's basis and date ("production run 15 Aug 2026, that day's extraction — reasoning only"), which is how the reader learns the two sides were not even looking at the same payload. `<Columns>` of two small cards works too when each reading needs a paragraph; the table is the default because it keeps the altitude low.

  **The change, literally.** "Add three lines to the instructions" left you asking where. Naming the file (`key/reader_brief.md`), the paragraph (the lapsed-cover convention), and showing the inserted text as a `<Diff>` in the drill-down answers it; nothing else in the batch's eleven cards names its destination either.

  **Cost of inaction.** A decision reader weighs the recommendation against doing nothing; one line makes that explicit and tells them whether the item is urgent.

  **Budget — and the cost.** The original card was 790 px tall and unreadable, so height is not the defect; shape is. The rewrite is about 1,700 px: twice the height, read once instead of three times. The lever to shorten it is the second balanced call at the end (when to show another party's reading). The default bound is that the visible card is read once, top to bottom, with no back-reference: about six fact rows, four reading rows, one quoted rule line, three lines of change. What the reader needs only if they doubt a fact — the extracted node, the gold's note, a model's full rationale, the key entry — goes in one `<Details>` directly above the `<ReviewChoice>`.

  **A batch of eleven.** The index table above the cards already makes the batch scannable; inside the cards, the fixed order is what makes eleven of them cheap — the reader learns once that the facts are the first table and the readings the second, and the eyebrow carries the subject so a scan of eyebrows reads as a list. Simpler items (the batch's C, D and I — a scope call, a scorer flag) take the question, the answer, one fact and the change; the order holds, the parts are defaults.

  **Tested and changed from the hypothesis.** The hypothesis listed the readings before the rule; the trace says the rule was the first unknown, so it moved up. The hypothesis had "what happens if he does nothing" last; it stays last but before the `<Details>`, so the visible card ends on the stakes rather than on evidence.
</Details>

  **`document-quality.md` §3 (Hierarchy)**, a new bullet after "One literal example beside each claim":

  > - **Facts as fields, not prose.** When a passage carries more than two or three facts the reader must hold together — values, dates, parties, sources — it is a compact `<Table>` or a field list, each fact with where it came from and as of when, and an unknown the reader will want stated as unknown. A paragraph of facts is re-parsed by every reader; a table is read once, and the source column answers "where does this come from" before it is asked.

  **`document-quality.md` §4 ("Three distinctions that are often got wrong")**, a fourth:

  > - **A before/after vs two readings.** `<FieldDiff>`'s panels are a change — current → proposed, as keyed → recommended — and a reader meets them as before/after whatever the labels say. Two parties' conclusions about the same record are readings, not a change: a `<Table>` with a column per party (its name, basis and date in the header, outcomes as badges) or a `<Columns>` pair, so no direction is implied.
